## §7-3 Who pays for a repair

The most common Service question a CSR gets, and the answer depends on which of the three states the equipment order is in.

| Situation | Who pays | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| **Still in the rental period** | UMS owns the equipment and is responsible for maintenance and repairs **at no charge** | [[§10-12.1]] |
| **Patient-owned — month 14 or later** | Medicare covers medically necessary repairs and parts. Deductible and coinsurance apply | [[§10-14]] |
| **Under manufacturer warranty** | Warranty claim, subject to approval and voiding conditions | [[§7-4]] |

> **Watch-out — check the rental status before saying anything about cost.** Telling a rental patient they owe money for a repair is wrong, and telling a patient-owned caller the repair is free is a promise Billing has to walk back. If you can't tell which applies, transfer to Service rather than guessing.

### §7-3.1 Where the table doesn't apply

Some categories sit outside the ordinary repair-payment rules. For these, "patient-owned, Medicare covers repairs" is the wrong answer.

| Category | What actually applies |
|---|---|
| **Oxygen equipment** | Repairs are not separately reimbursed. Servicing runs on a maintenance-and-servicing payment every 6 months instead — [[§10-12.2]] |
| **Frequent and substantial servicing items** — ventilators, E0471 | Not separately reimbursed. The item is never owned, and servicing is part of the monthly rental — [[§10-2.2]] |
| **Anything under warranty** | Parts and labour covered by a manufacturer's or supplier's warranty cannot be billed to the patient or to Medicare |

### §7-3.2 Diagnostic visits

A technician can be sent to evaluate equipment, but technicians are normally dispatched to perform a **confirmed** service or repair.

> **Policy — when a diagnostic visit is charged.** A visit to diagnose equipment carries a **$75 out-of-pocket charge** when either:
>
> - the patient **refuses to provide the requested photos or video** of their equipment and the issue, or
> - the equipment was **recently found to be operational by one of our technicians**, and is again determined to be fully functional.
>
> Say this before the visit is booked, not after — and ask for the photos or video first.

## §7-4 Manufacturer warranty

| Detail | |
|---|---|
| Typical period | **1 year** in most cases |
| Variation | Some items are shorter or longer — 6 months, 2 years |
| Process | Service **requests** warranty coverage, and the **manufacturer** decides whether it applies |
| Voiding | Certain conditions void a warranty. Water damage is the common one — a PWC left out in the rain likely cannot be serviced under warranty |

A CSR's role is to route the call, not to assess whether the warranty applies.

## §7-5 Respiratory equipment

### §7-5.1 Ventilators — highest priority

> **Critical.** A patient on a ventilator is on life support. **Any issue involving a vent patient is handled with the highest priority and urgency.**
>
> Service requests go through the Service Q. If no answer, email **both** `respiratory@universalmedsupply.com` **and** `service@universalmedsupply.com`, **and** notify an RT — `[ROLE: Respiratory Therapists]`. See [[§3-10]].
>
> After hours, a ventilator issue means **dispatching the on-call RT immediately** — don't troubleshoot. See [[§9-3]].

### §7-5.2 What Service handles and what stays with CSR

> **Policy** — Complex respiratory service requests that previously went to respiratory therapists exclusively now route through the Service Q. **Oxygen concentrator servicing remains with CSR.**

The Service team may proactively contact patients about concentrator servicing and initiate it, but a patient calling back about that is not a handoff — concentrator servicing is still CSR work. See [[§8-6]].

Trilogy and cough assist issues go to the Service Q. If no answer, email `respiratory@universalmedsupply.com` and `service@universalmedsupply.com` and notify `[ROLE: Respiratory Therapists]`.

### §7-5.3 CPAP set-up and instruction

Transfer to Service so they can create the ticket. Field Ops then schedules a respiratory therapist.

## §7-6 Power mobility service

A patient **reporting a problem** with their PMD comes to the Service Q. Once Service has arranged the repair, the visit itself is scheduled through the Field Ops-Power Q — see [[§6-1.1]].

### §7-6.1 Chargers and batteries

UMS stocks some PMD chargers and batteries in the office warehouse, available for out-of-pocket purchase depending on the model.

> **Policy — this is available to non-customers too.** If a caller has no account with us and did not receive their PMD from us, Service may still be able to quote an out-of-pocket cost for a replacement charger or battery, **provided it is a PMD model we carry**.

Don't turn these callers away on the grounds that they aren't our patient. Transfer to the Service Q to check the model and availability.

### §7-6.2 Loaner equipment

Only discuss a loaner if the caller asks for one. If they do ask, it depends on why they're without equipment:

| Situation | Loaner |
|---|---|
| **Patient-owned equipment is being repaired** | Possibly. Service must confirm |
| **Still in the rental period** | No separate loaner — UMS is already responsible for the repair |
| **Waiting for a new device to be approved or delivered** | No |

Even where a loaner may be possible, don't promise one — tell the caller you're checking with Service, not that one is coming.

### §7-6.3 PMD pick-up requests — check for a repair first

> **Policy — understand the reason before creating a pick-up ticket.** PMD pick-ups are more complicated than other equipment, so the reason matters. Discomfort and faults both come to Service — the decision diagram and the full table are in [[§6-9.2]].

## §7-7 Equipment swap-outs

When a patient needs to exchange what they received for a different version of the item, the route depends on **one question: does the change require additional documentation from the MDO?** — a different or additional HCPCS code, or a new MRx, for example.

{{diagram:swap-out}}

### §7-7.1 No additional documentation required

Transfer to Service. They create a **service ticket for the swap-out** and handle it from there. Nothing else is needed.

### §7-7.2 Additional documentation required

This is a coordinated two-sided process, and you start both sides.

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Create a pick-up ticket** for the item the patient already received |
| 2 | **Note on the ticket** that it was created for a swap-out, and that pick-up must be **coordinated with the processing and delivery of the new item** |
| 3 | **Email the relevant intake department** explaining the swap-out, so they can request the updated documents from the MDO and initiate the new order |

> **Watch-out — the email is not optional.** The pick-up ticket alone does not start the new order. Without the email, the intake team has no trigger to request the updated documentation, and the patient ends up with their equipment collected and nothing replacing it.

> **Watch-out — do not let the pick-up run ahead of the delivery.** The coordination note exists so the patient isn't left without equipment while the new order waits on documentation. Say so explicitly on the ticket.

> **Script — patient asking to swap an item.** "Let me look at what you have and what you're asking for. If the change needs updated paperwork from your doctor's office, we'll get that started and make sure the swap is coordinated so you're not without your equipment in between."

## §7-10 Lost, stolen, and irreparably damaged equipment

Medicare will not pay for replacement equipment within the five-year reasonable useful lifetime **unless the item was lost, stolen, or irreparably damaged** — see [[§10-12.5]]. Those are the three narrow exceptions, and they're handled differently.

### §7-10.1 Irreparable damage

Two separate questions get confused here, and they have different answers.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Can the manufacturer cover it?** | Usually no. Irreparable damage is typically **not** a manufacturer defect, and the damage itself has often voided the warranty — water damage and outdoor use being the common causes |
| **Can insurance cover a replacement?** | Possibly — if the damage came from a **specific accident or a natural disaster**, such as a fire or a flood. That's what Medicare means by irreparable damage, and it's one of the three SOS exceptions |

Damage from neglect or misuse isn't irreparable damage in that sense. A PWC left out in the rain has usually voided its warranty **and** doesn't qualify for a covered replacement — it's generally the patient's responsibility ([[§7-10.2]]). A chair destroyed in a house fire is the kind of case the exception is for.

> **Note — the incident has to be documented.** Medicare expects records describing what happened — a police report, an insurance claim report, or similar.

> **Watch-out** — Don't tell a patient either answer on the call. Whether damage is irreparable is Service's determination, and whether that produces a covered replacement is Billing's. What a CSR can say is that Service will assess it.

### §7-10.2 Lost or stolen equipment

> **Policy — a police report is required.** Where equipment is lost or damaged **through no fault of the patient**, insurance may agree to pay for a replacement. To initiate that process, UMS requires **a copy of a filed police report** covering the incident.

Without the report, the process doesn't start. This is the first thing to establish on the call.

> **Watch-out — the police report is required to attempt to get a replacement.** Filing a report doesn't guarantee a covered replacement; it's what allows the request to be submitted. Don't tell a patient that a police report means they'll get a new device.

> **Script — patient says their equipment was stolen.** "I'm sorry that happened. Insurance will sometimes cover a replacement when equipment is lost or stolen through no fault of your own, but to start that process we'd need a copy of a police report for the incident. If you haven't filed one yet, that's the first step. Once you have it, send it over and our team can look at what's possible."

A shipped package the carrier shows as delivered, that the patient says never arrived, is different — no police report is needed. See [[§7-2]].

## §7-11 How long a service request takes

> **Policy — turnaround varies widely and should not be quoted as a range.** Smaller issues may be completed within a few days. Requests needing **parts ordered from the manufacturer** may take several weeks, and logistical delays at that stage are outside UMS's control.

> **Script — patient asking how long.** "It depends on what the technician finds. Straightforward issues are often handled within a few days. If a part has to be ordered from the manufacturer it can take longer — sometimes several weeks — and that part of the timeline isn't in our hands. Service will be in touch once they know what's needed."

This is also why the internal notes rule below matters: a tracking date is not a service date.

## §7-12 Reading Service notes

> **Policy — "(For internal use only)".** The Service team marks some notes this way to indicate that this information **should not be relayed to the patient**.

The clearest example: a tracking number for a repair part may be shipping to a **warehouse**, not the patient's home. Service timing therefore won't match what the tracking suggests, because the part still has to be verified and a technician scheduled.

## §7-13 Outbound service calls

The Service team sometimes calls patients to ask whether their equipment needs servicing — partly lead generation for service and repair work, partly a customer-satisfaction check.

A patient calling back about one of these is **responding to our outreach**, not necessarily reporting an issue. If that seems to be the case, confirm whether they're experiencing any issues with their equipment, and if not, simply include that in your notes. If they report an issue, route to the Service Q.

## §7-14 FAQ

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| My manual wheelchair didn't come with a seatbelt. Can I get one? | Standard wheelchair orders don't include one. It can be added with a new referral from the MDO, and intake will work on it. See [[§2-4.3]] |
| You can no longer service my PMD — is there another company? | Suggest they contact a local DME repair service. For insurance to pay for the repair, that provider must be enrolled with, or in network for, the patient's plan |
| Can I buy a PMD charger or battery? | Possibly, depending on the model and warehouse stock, at out-of-pocket cost. This applies even if the PMD didn't come from us. See [[§7-6.1]] |
| Can you move my hospital bed? | Generally no, with limited exceptions. See [[§7-9]] |
| How long is the manufacturer's warranty? | Usually 1 year, though some items differ. Warranty coverage requires a request and approval, and some conditions void it — water damage being the common one. See [[§7-4]] |
| Will my repair cost me anything? | Depends on whether the equipment is still in the rental period, patient-owned, or under warranty. See [[§7-3]] |
| Can I get a loaner while mine is repaired? | Possibly, if the equipment is patient-owned — Service confirms. Don't raise it unless asked. See [[§7-6.2]]. |
| The tracking says my part arrives tomorrow, so why isn't my repair scheduled? | Parts often ship to our warehouse rather than the patient's home. Service verifies the part and schedules by technician availability. See [[§7-12]] |
