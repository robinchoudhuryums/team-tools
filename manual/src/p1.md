# Part 1 — Call Handling


> **About this part.** Every other part is organized by department. This one is organized by **what the caller says**. It carries the things that apply to every call regardless of which department owns the order: where to route, who you may speak to, what you may not do, and how to handle the calls that are hard for reasons other than complexity.
>
> Like Part 0, this part appears in full in every standalone extract.

## §1-2 Do's and don'ts

Consolidated from across the manual. These are the ones with money, coverage, or legal consequences.

| ✓ Do | ✗ Don't | Because | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Say coverage is confirmed once verification is complete | Promise coverage or approval | Coverage depends on verification; authorization is the payer's decision | [[§10-21.2]] |
| Give an estimate, and say the final amount is known once the claim processes | Quote an exact dollar amount as final | Approved amounts vary by fee schedule and delivery address | [[§10-13.7]] |
| Explain the fee, and route a waiver question to Billing | Waive or reduce an upgrade fee | Three waiver rules exist and CSRs apply none of them by discretion | [[§10-16.1]] |
| Route a QMB patient's bill to Billing | Collect Medicare cost-sharing from a QMB patient | **Federal law**, not a courtesy | [[§10-6.3]] |
| Say the amount is confirmed once the claim processes | Ask for payment before the claim has processed | The amount isn't known until the remittance arrives. Exceptions: upgrade fees and OOP | [[§10-13.4]] |
| Give only the date the system shows | Promise a delivery date not in the system | | [[§0-2.3]] |
| Say the parts are on the way, and Service schedules the visit | Quote a tracking date as a service date | Parts often ship to the warehouse, not the patient | [[§6-12]] |
| Say it depends on rental status and warranty, and Service confirms | Quote a repair cost | Depends on rental status and warranty | [[§6-3]] |
| Answer from the specialists' notes; route anything beyond them to the Compliance Specialist | Decide compliance where the notes are silent | Beyond the notes, it's the Compliance Specialist's determination | [[§3-12]] |
| Leave the determination to Service | Decide whether damage is irreparable | Service's determination, with billing consequences | [[§6-10.1]] |
| Answer if the caller asks, and say Service decides | Mention a loaner unprompted | Availability is limited and Service decides; raising it creates an expectation | [[§6-6.2]] |
| Explain that oxygen stays a rental | Tell an oxygen patient they'll own the concentrator | They won't | [[§10-13.2]] |
| Say the team is reviewing the denial, including for a possible appeal | Tell a patient a denial will be overturned | | [[§10-18.3]] |
| Have the referral sent so the specific plan can be checked | Say "we take UHC / Aetna / BCBS" | Network status is plan-by-plan | [[§10-11]] |
| Notate first, and tell the receiving team what the caller needs | Cold transfer a caller | The receiving team starts from nothing and the caller repeats themselves | [[§0-12.4]] |
| Follow the on-call schedule and its backups | Contact off-schedule on-call staff | | [[§8-4]] |
| Tell the patient to ask their physician | Change a patient's oxygen flow rate | Physician direction only | [[§7-8.3]] |

> **Watch-out — the pattern.** Almost every item here is a case where saying yes feels helpful in the moment. The cost lands on someone else later: Billing walking back a promise, a technician arriving to a job that can't be done, a patient told they'd own equipment they don't.

## §1-3 Who you may speak to

> **Critical — verify before discussing anything.** Don't discuss a patient's information until the caller has been verified. A caller other than the patient may be told **order and billing details** once they confirm their **relationship to the patient**, the patient's **full name**, and the patient's **date of birth and/or home address**. See [[§10-23]].

| Caller | What you may do |
|---|---|
| The patient | Everything, after verification |
| A **documented authorized contact** on the account | Everything, after verifying the patient's details and confirming they're the listed contact |
| Someone with a signed **AOR** on file | As above |
| A family member or caregiver **not** on the account | Once verified as above — **order and billing details**, including balances and taking a payment. If they can't be verified, take information *from* them only |
| A facility nurse or MDO staff calling about the order | Discuss the order. Verify who they are and note it |
| An insurance representative | Requesting authorization information → the intake team's authorization members. Giving a decision → record it and email intake. See [[§0-12.2]] and [[§0-12.3]] |
| Someone claiming power of attorney | As for a family member |

> **Watch-out — before verification, information flows one way.** A caller who isn't verified yet can still *give* you information — a new address, for instance — but not receive it. Once verified, a daughter can be told where her father's order stands and what he owes, and can make a payment for him.

> **Script — caller can't be verified.** "I want to help, but I'm not able to go over the order without confirming a few details first — that's part of the HIPAA guidelines we follow. What I can do is take down what you've told me and have someone reach out to [patient] directly."

### §1-3.1 Adding an authorized contact

Transaction Workflow → **`Patient`** tab → **Phone Manager** → enter and save the details.

> **Note — why this is permitted.** HIPAA allows a covered entity to share information with a family member or other person involved in a patient's care, using professional judgment. Confirming the caller's relationship and the patient's details is how that judgment is exercised here.

## §1-4 Difficult calls

### §1-4.1 A caller in distress

This is the **single canonical emergency instruction** for the whole manual. Every other part points
here rather than restating it.

> **Critical — emergencies come first.** Advise the caller to **hang up and dial 9-1-1** if **either**:
>
> - the caller says they need emergency help **within the next few minutes**, or describes anything
>   comparable; **or**
> - **you believe** a medical emergency is taking place, whatever the caller has said.
>
> Do not troubleshoot, do not place them on hold, and do not take details first.

The distinguishing question is whether the patient is in **immediate physical distress right now** —
not whether the situation is urgent or stressful. Patients sometimes describe something as an
emergency when it isn't, unintentionally or to get faster service.

> **Policy — when in doubt, err toward 9-1-1.** Being wrong in that direction costs a call. Being
> wrong in the other direction has no floor.

### §1-4.2 An angry caller

Most anger on these calls is about one of a small number of things: a bill that wasn't expected, a delay nobody explained, or equipment that doesn't work.

| ✓ Do | ✗ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let them finish before responding | Interrupt to correct a detail |
| Acknowledge the actual problem — "you've been waiting three weeks and nobody's called you" | Apologize for something you haven't confirmed |
| Tell them what you're going to do, specifically | Promise an outcome to end the call — see [[§1-2]] |
| Say what you can't do and why, plainly | Blame another department to the caller |
| Offer a supervisor if they ask — see [[§1-4.4]] | Wait until they demand one |

> **Watch-out — don't concede a disputed fact to calm someone.** The most common version is agreeing that nobody explained the 13-month billing. Check for the signed PRF first — see [[§5-6.3]]. Sympathy about the frustration is free; conceding the fact is not.

> **Policy — de-escalation expectations**
>
> | Expectation | Standard |
> |---|---|
> | **Acknowledge first** | Name the caller's problem back to them before explaining anything |
> | **Holds** | Say why before placing them on hold |
> | **One next step** | End with one specific action, and when they'll hear back |
> | **Don't go in circles arguing facts** | Verify a disputed point, then present the facts. If they aren't willing to accept the facts, don't get stuck repeating the same thing — focus on the resolution step instead |
> | **Second supervisor request** | Escalate per [[§1-4.4]] — don't keep trying to resolve it yourself |
> | **No supervisor available** | Tell the caller, say you'll pass it on immediately, and that they'll get a callback within **24–48 hours** |
> | **Note it** | Record the concern in the caller's words, and exactly what you committed to |

### §1-4.3 An abusive caller

Verbal abuse means cursing, threatening, or otherwise inappropriate conduct directed at you. Frustration and raised voices are not abuse — see [[§1-4.2]].

> **Policy — warn first, then disconnect.** A call is never disconnected without a warning.
>
> **1. Warn.** Calmly and clearly tell the caller that if they can continue the conversation in an appropriate manner we will be happy to help them — and that if they continue the inappropriate behavior, we will be forced to disconnect the call.
>
> **2. Disconnect.** If the abuse continues, inform them that because they have continued inappropriately you are now ending the call, then disconnect.

> **Script — the warning.** "We only succeed if our patients are happy, so I want the same thing as you and to help you with this — but I need to ask that we keep the conversation respectful. If we can do that, I'm happy to assist you, but if it continues disrespectfully, I'll be forced to end the call."

> **Script — the disconnect.** "Because the verbal abuse has continued, I'm forced to end the call."

> **Watch-out — the warning is not optional and neither is the second statement.** Hanging up without warning, or hanging up silently after one, leaves the caller with a legitimate complaint about us. Both statements are what make the disconnection defensible.

Note the account afterward: what was said, that a warning was given, and that the call was disconnected.

### §1-4.4 Requests for a supervisor

> **Policy — gather before you escalate.** Collect the preliminary details first, then find someone. Do not transfer a caller to a supervisor cold, or without being able to explain the caller's concern.

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Collect the **patient name and date of birth** so the account can be found |
| 2 | **Understand the caller's specific concern.** Not "they're upset" — what they actually want resolved |
| 3 | Contact the **relevant team's supervisor or lead** — the department that owns the issue |
| 4 | If that isn't possible, contact **your own supervisor or lead** |
| 5 | If neither is available, tell the caller that plainly, pass it on immediately by email, and let them know to expect a **callback within 24–48 hours** |

> **Watch-out — step 2 is the one that gets skipped.** A supervisor who receives a cold transfer has to make the caller explain everything a second time, which is exactly what an already-frustrated caller experiences as being "given the run-around."

> **Script — no supervisor available.** "Unfortunately the relevant supervisor isn't available right now, so I'm going to send them a message about this right away, and you can expect a callback within 24 to 48 hours."

## §1-5 When a patient has died

This call usually comes from a family member within days of the death, and often the caller is the one handling everything.

{{diagram:death-call}}

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| 1 | Express condolences. Don't move straight to logistics |
| 2 | Note the **date of death** on the account |
| 3 | Check whether each item is **patient-owned** — paid in full, or past the point where ownership transferred |
| 4 | **Patient-owned:** tell the caller the equipment is theirs to keep, donate or dispose of as they wish. There is nothing to collect |
| 5 | **Not patient-owned:** confirm the best contact and the pick-up address, and create a pick-up ticket — the contact won't be the patient. Field Ops will verify and call back within **24–48 hours**. If the family would rather pay the remaining cost and keep the items, transfer to Billing |
| 6 | A verified family member can be told the balance — see [[§1-3]]. Route unresolved invoice questions and disputes to Billing |

> **Policy — billing stops as of the date of death.** Any month billed after that is corrected by Billing. A final invoice for prior months may still be owed by the estate. See [[§10-15]].

> **Watch-out — don't promise a collection.** Creating the ticket isn't the same as arranging the pick-up. Say it has been requested and that someone will be in touch — see [[§5-9]].

> **Note — oxygen is never patient-owned.** A concentrator always comes back, however long the patient had it. See [[§7-8.1]].

> **Script — equipment to be collected.** "I'm very sorry for your loss. I'll note this on the account so you don't have to explain it to anyone else here. I've put in a request for the equipment to be collected — our field team will verify it and call you back within 24 to 48 hours with an update. If you'd rather keep any of it and pay what's left, I can connect you with our billing team."

> **Note — donating equipment.** We don't have a list of organizations to refer families to, but many non-profit organizations will gladly accept — and often pick up — donated DME for the many people who need it.

> **Script — patient-owned equipment.** "I'm very sorry for your loss. I'll note this on the account. That equipment was paid for, so it belongs to the family now — you're welcome to keep it, donate it, or dispose of it however suits you. There's nothing we need to collect."

## §1-7 Complaints

> **Policy — this is a Medicare supplier standard, not an internal courtesy.** Suppliers must answer beneficiary questions and respond to complaints, and maintain documentation of those contacts. Suppliers must also have a complaint resolution protocol for complaints relating to the supplier standards, with records kept.

### §1-7.1 What a complaint record must contain

Per the supplier standards, a complaint record must include:

| Field |
|---|
| Beneficiary name |
| Address |
| Telephone number |
| A summary of the complaint |
| Any actions taken to resolve it |

> **Watch-out — collect these on the call.** Going back to a complainant to ask for their address is a second bad experience. Take all five while you have them.

### §1-7.2 Calls from Medicare

> **Critical — treat these with extra sensitivity.** If the caller is from **traditional Medicare or 1-800-MEDICARE**, and the call concerns a **complaint or an issue**, get it **directly to a supervisor**.

This is not a normal inbound call. A complaint reaching us through Medicare rather than from the patient means it has already escalated outside the company.

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identify that the caller is from Medicare — don't assume any inbound caller is the patient or a provider |
| 2 | Take the patient details and the nature of the complaint or issue |
| 3 | **Route directly to a supervisor** — see [[§1-4.4]], but do not work through the steps first |
| 4 | Record it as a complaint per [[§1-7.1]] |

> **Watch-out — don't try to resolve it on the call.** The instinct is to fix it and close the loop. A Medicare-originated complaint needs a supervisor on it from the start, with a record, regardless of how simple the underlying issue looks.

### §1-7.3 Routing

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Email the relevant department**, using the standard subject format with **Complaint** or **Escalation** as the update type — `Complaint: Jane Smith 123456`. See [[§0-11.2]] |
| 2 | The complaint is then added manually to the escalation tracking spreadsheet, which captures all five required fields |
| 3 | **Resolution is owned by the department manager** for the department the complaint concerns |

> **Policy — acknowledge within 48 hours.** Resolution time varies with the complexity of the concern and no single target fits every case. What is fixed is the front end: the escalation must be **acknowledged, or a first action taken, within 48 hours**.

Medicare-originated complaints go to a supervisor first — see [[§1-7.2]].

> **Note** — if a patient asks for the Medicare supplier standards, they're published at [universalmedsupply.com/supplier-standards](https://universalmedsupply.com/supplier-standards/). Send the link.

> **Watch-out — include all five fields.** The standard CSR notation template can capture these fields — just be sure you confirm all the required complaint details are included: name, address, telephone number, a summary of the complaint, and any actions already taken — see [[§1-7.1]] and [[§0-11.1]]. The template has no address line, so put the address in **Issue**. The tracking spreadsheet is populated from what you send, and anything missing has to be chased back from the complainant, which is a second bad experience.

## §1-8 Language access

> **Policy — Spanish is the only non-English language UMS officially supports.** Use the Spanish Queue or the Spanish inbox — never transfer to a Spanish-speaking agent directly to get around them. A direct transfer isn't queued or tracked, so nothing confirms the request was completed.

This applies both ways: a caller who needs a Spanish speaker, and an employee trying to reach out to a Spanish-speaking patient.

### §1-8.1 Inbound — the caller needs a Spanish speaker

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Call the **Spanish Queue** |
| 2 | If someone answers — transfer the caller |
| 3 | If no one is available — **transfer the caller to the Spanish Queue Voicemail** |

The voicemail plays an automated message in Spanish asking the caller to leave the patient name, date of birth, and reason for the call. A bilingual team member calls back as soon as they are able.

> **Note — no email on an inbound call.** You won't be able to send the details, because you can't understand what the caller is telling you. The voicemail is what collects them. This is the one case where the transfer is the whole step.

### §1-8.2 Outbound — you need to reach a Spanish-speaking patient

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Ask the patient first whether someone is with them** who speaks English or can translate. Often there is |
| 2 | If not, call the **Spanish Queue** |
| 3 | If no one is available — **send the email** to `spanishcalls@universalmedsupply.com` and tell the patient we will call back when a Spanish translator is available |

> **Watch-out — on an outbound call the email is required.** You have the patient details and the reason for the call, so there's no voicemail step doing that work for you. Sending nothing means the callback never gets queued.

### §1-8.3 What the outbound email must contain

Send to `spanishcalls@universalmedsupply.com`, using the standard subject format — `Spanish Callback: Maria Garcia 456789`. See [[§0-11.2]].

| Field |
|---|
| Patient details |
| Transaction number |
| Reason for the call — what needs to be discussed or completed |
| Requested callback timeframe, if applicable |
